## The premise, unchanged

You live in a small town that is wary of anyone who keeps going down. In the top
left corner of the town there is a stair. You cannot help going down it. The
descent is ordinary work — keys, monsters, a floor at a time — and at the bottom
of it there is something the town sealed away forty years ago, and it is not a
monster. It is a room that was kept for people who had nowhere else to sleep.

You go down. You come back up. You carry the coal home.

## Section 1 — The door that was closed

The town is small and it is wary of you. Nobody is cruel. They simply do not
invite.

In the top left corner there is a stair, and beside it a door — the Blue Door,
blue the way deep water is blue, the colour it keeps when a thing has been
underneath for a long time. It is the door you come up out of. It is not locked.
It has not been locked for years. But it was, once, and everybody in town knows
it, and nobody has ever said so.

Forty years ago it was a severe winter. Nineteen people met — nineteen, out of a
town that can be walked in an hour — and they agreed on a thing that was not
cruel and was not kind. They agreed to close the travellers' room. The room went
unused. The beds went to other uses. The food and the fuel and the good blankets
were counted out among themselves and kept, because a severe winter is a thing
you can starve in, and nineteen frightened people did the arithmetic and got an
answer they could live with.

Underneath the common hearth, the passage was sealed. The hearth was banked — the
fire kept low and slow and covered, so it would last, because that was the whole
point. Kindness was on the fire's list. Kindness was what got banked.

They thought kindness was something they could run out of.

They were wrong about that, and it is the whole story, and it takes four rooms to
say.

**How it plays.** The town is the first thing that feels different, and it never
becomes friendly. It becomes *less careful*. The first sign is not a speech. It
is a door held open that did not used to be held open. The player should notice it
and be allowed to walk past it.

## Section 2 — Down

The stair is not a punishment. It is a job.

Three floors down, a great pale moth sleeps on the landing. She has a key. You
wake her; you take the key; she goes back to sleep. Further down, an old mushroom
in a doorway. Further down, a stag beetle in a great deal of armour. Each one is
asleep. Each one is holding something small and important. This is the shape of
the whole descent: not a gauntlet, a household.

Nobody down here wants anything from you.

**The letter.** In an open chest on the first floor, a letter folded small and
tied with thread:

> Whoever finds this: I did not go missing. I went down on purpose, and I meant
> to come back. Tell the keeper I kept my word about the cakes.

It is signed with a name you do not know.

Not the Spell Keeper. She came back — the town knows that, though nobody says it
plainly — and this is not her hand, and she would not write *I meant to come
back* about herself. It is **Oren**, the Fisher's brother. He went down some
years ago. Nobody has seen him since. He is not here now, and he does not
appear, and the draft does not say what became of him.

**Oren's disappearance remains unresolved.** That is the honest state of it, and
the ending does not tidy it.

**The coin.** One coin, set face down on the stone of the third floor.
Deliberate, not dropped. You leave it as it is. It reads like someone's habit,
not yours.

**The candle.** On the fifth floor, a candle burned down to nothing, and a note
pinned under it: *If you are reading this, you went down too. Good. Someone
should.* Nobody sat here a long while and never came back up — or somebody did,
and the floor is being kind about it. The draft does not decide which.

**Merrin's coat.** There is a man called Merrin, and there is a coat on a hook.
Someone kept it. Someone has been keeping it.

[#111's line for the coat on the hook, verbatim — to be restored from the branch.]

What the draft says instead, and no further: **Merrin went down voluntarily,
before the passage was sealed, on purpose, with his reasons of his own.** He was
not appointed to anything. He was not a jailer. Nobody sent him and nobody sent
him home. He went, and the coat stayed on the hook, and **his fate remains
unresolved**. The coat is not a clue to a death. It is a clue that somebody still
owns it.

**The cat's opinion.** The cat has one about all of this, and it is the same
opinion it has about everything, which is that this is your business and it is
beneath the cat. The cat is not a guide. The cat is a cat who permits you to
live here, and permits it on condition that you are occasionally ridiculous.

## Section 3 — The banked hearth

The last room has a Cellar King in it and a ring of old red wax.

He is not the story. He is the door. He carries a crown seal, and when the seal
is broken the King falls and there is a chest under him and a note folded on top
of the gems, and the note says this:

> You made it down. Good. Sit a moment; the stairs are long.
>
> The old tavern kept one room for travellers. The town was afraid of what a kind
> room might cost, so it put a fire over the room, and a seal over the fire, and
> called that safe.
>
> Nothing down here was ever as unkind as the sealing. Take what you found. Take
> it up. Kindness is lighter than it looks, and it knows the way home.

The note stays exactly as it is. **This draft does not say which of the two the
town meant** — whether they meant to put a fire over the room, or whether they
banked a fire to last and then left it untended. Those are different crimes and
one of them is a bureaucratic accident, and the pack already says both things
out loud in two different places. That question is not Claude's to settle and it
is not settled here.

What *is* settled, because Rylee settled it, is the shape of the fire. **The Breeze
burned later, by accident. Nobody set it.** No arson. No villain standing in the
snow nineteen years later. A banked hearth that was banked to keep people warm
through a winter, left a little too long, in a building nobody was using, in a
town that had stopped talking about why.

That is the reveal, and it is the one that should land:

**What the town put a fire over was its own kindness.** The room was not
dangerous. It was the only thing the old tavern ever did wrong. Nineteen people
sealed a mercy because they had worked out that mercy has a cost, and they
wanted to be the ones who did not pay it.

You bring the coal up.

The town does not thank you. The town gets a little warmer, slowly, across many
returns, through small practical acts of hospitality that nobody announces and
nobody explains: a lamp moved to a darker street, a door propped, a pot on that
was not on before. Improvements are staggered. The town never becomes a good
place all at once. The world remains imperfect — there are still people in it who
were not at the meeting, who think you are odd, who never bring it up.

## Section 4 — The kept room

**This is the ending, and it is also the spine of the whole game.**

There is no final boss after the King. There is no speech in the square. There
is no kindness score and no moral explanation and nothing to unlock. The final
section ends at the Blue Door and the kept room.

The kept room is the room under the hearth. You unseal it. It is empty.

It is empty and there are **six places set**.

Six. Not five, not a dozen, not a crowd. Six places, laid out, at a table, as
though someone expected six people and is early. You do not find out who they
were for. The draft does not name them. Six is the number, and the six are not
explained, because the room is not a puzzle and the ending is not a ledger.

**The kept room remains revisitable.** This is the part that matters most, and
it is the part that is easiest to get wrong. It is not a cutscene and it is not
an unlock. It is a room you can come back to, as often as you like, forever,
with no timer and nothing counting down.

**Nothing in the kept room ages.** Not the bread, not the blanket, not the
guests. If you brought someone down here, they are safe here in a way that is
otherwise not safe. There is no consequence to that. There is no hidden cost and
no surprise. It is a warm room.

**Bramble comes home.** Bramble is at the cottage, and Bramble is fine, and
Bramble can walk down to the kept room and sit in one of the six places whenever
they like. He does not age. He does not suffer. He has a peaceful life at the
cottage and he spends part of it in a room with a table and a fire, and that is
the whole of what happened to him. That part matters.

### The room after the first visit

Once the kept room is open, coming back to it is optional and unhurried. There
are at most **three** signs of welcome across the whole game, one per optional
return, and no more:

1. **First return:** a blanket on the back of a chair that was not there. Nobody
   says where it came from. It is warm.
2. **Second return:** bread on the table, still warm, from a town that is
   somehow still baking. And different people at the table than last time —
   two of them, this time, sitting like they have been there a while. They do
   not speak to you. They are not waiting for you.
3. **Third return:** a small gift on your place. Not a reward. Something someone
   thought was nice — a pressed flower, a coin from a country that is not this
   one, a child's drawing of a room they have never been in.

After the third, the room is simply there. It does not escalate. It does not
need you.

**Hard limits, because the moment these slip the thing stops working.** The
kept room is never: a chore, a check-in, a daily task, a thing with a deadline,
a reward for returning, a shop, a hub with quests in it, a crowd, a hub, or a
place the game asks you to come back to. The player can keep exploring
afterwards, forever, and the room will still be empty and warm and will not ask
anything of them.

**The last line of the game is someone making room at the table.**

A cold, wet traveller arrives. It is late and it has been raining and they have
nowhere to be. Nobody makes a speech. Nobody confesses anything. Somebody just
pulls out a chair.

> **"There's room."**

That is the ending. The player can close the game there, or keep playing, or go
down the stair again, and the room will be there when they get back.

## The two devices #111 had, kept

**Residents are lines, not geometry.** Every resident in this draft is a short
line said once, in the room they belong to. Nobody has a quest. Nobody has a
schedule the player can learn. Nobody blocks a door. The town is made out of what
people say when you walk past them, and what they do not say, which is more.

**An object that means more the second time you read it.** The letter is the
workhorse. Read the first time, it is a note from somebody who went missing.
Read it after you have met the Spell Keeper and understood she came back from her
own descent years ago, it becomes a promise about *cakes* — a small, ridiculous,
specific promise made in the dark, kept by a man who then went down anyway. It
means more. Nothing about it changed. You did.

The coin, face down, works the other way: the first time it is a coin. The second
time, someone else's habit, someone else's superstition, laid down on purpose and
left. It reads like someone's habit, not yours.

## What this draft deliberately does not say

**One sentence, held back for a part 2: whether anyone the town sealed in ever
came back up.**

The draft says that the Spell Keeper came back. It says that Oren went down and
is not accounted for, and that **Oren's disappearance remains unresolved**. It
says that Merrin went down on purpose before the seal and that **Merrin's fate
remains unresolved**. It sets six places at a table and does not say who they are
for.

It never puts those three facts together in prose. It never asks the player to
put them together either. The held-back sentence is the one that would have to
come next, and part 2 is where it belongs — because the answer, when it comes,
ought to cost something, and it cannot cost anything if it is spent in the first
hour in a room with six empty places.

Everything else unresolved stays unresolved. That is not a gap being hidden. That
is the shape.
