## The premise, in one sentence

You live in the cottage at the end of the road because nobody else wanted it, there is
a stair in the corner of the kitchen, and you keep going down because somebody should.

**Why you go down, and it is a small reason.** You are not brave. You are not chosen.
Nobody sent you. The stair is *in your own kitchen*, so it is not a quest, it is just
there when you make tea. Under the door there is a note in a hand you don't know:
*you keep going down. someone should.* You are not doing it for the town. You are
doing it because the town is quiet and cold and grey, the Spell Keeper keeps a cake
back for whoever comes up the stair, and you would very much like to be the one who is
glad to see. It turns into the biggest thing you have ever done without ever once
feeling like a hero about it.

**What you are carrying up is heat.** Not a relic, not a crown. The town has a cold
hearth and does not know why. Everything below is a piece of the town's own fire,
brought home.

## The town

Small. One road. The cottage, the tavern, the Trader's stall, a temple you wake up in,
and the black gap where a building used to stand. People are polite and nobody will say
the thing.

### Who is there, and what each of them wants

| Who | Wants | What is in the way |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Spell Keeper** | For her to be *named*, eventually, as one of the ones who went down. She has known how this ends since before you arrived. | The town has decided she is a shopkeeper. Nobody asks her a real question. |
| **The Tavern Regular** | To hear the truth about the fire told out loud, in the tavern, with people in it. He sat at the same table for twenty years. | He was a boy when The Breeze burned, and he has decided not to have been a coward about it. |
| **The Dwarf Smith** | Work. He is the best mender in the district and there is nothing left to mend. | His forge is banked by three and cold by four, every day, and he will not say why he does it early. |
| **The Fisher** | A full net, and his brother Oren home. Oren went down once and did not come up, and the town has been gentle about it in a way that clearly means *ask no questions*. | The river runs warmer and thinner every year. He blames the weather. He does not quite believe it. |
| **The Gardener** | Something to grow that isn't food. She talks to the beans and means it. | Every bed is thinner than the year before. She has started giving seedlings away to people who don't want them. |
| **The Trader** | A full stall. He buys what you bring up and asks no questions at all, which is a kindness. | He is the only person in town who is not wary, because he never asks. |
| **The Hearth Cat** | The quiet to go on being quiet. She owns the cottage. You live here at her pleasure. | She has decided about you, and we do not find out what. |

### What the town is losing, slowly

**The fire.** Not a metaphor — an actual thing. The old tavern's fire was the town's
fire. It warmed the smith, and the bread, and the beds of anyone with nowhere to sleep.
When The Breeze burned, the fire went with it, and nobody rebuilt a fire hot enough to
matter. It is not that the town is dying. It is that the town has gone *cold and
quiet*, in the specific way of a house where nobody wants to be the first to speak.

Every day costs a little more: a colder forge, a smaller catch, a thinner bed, a road
nobody walks after dark. Nobody is cruel. The town is **wary of anyone who goes down**,
which is the stupidest possible thing to be wary of, because everything warm in this
story is coming up from below with somebody's hands on it.

**And here is what the town did, and what it did not do.** Forty years ago it was a
severe winter. Nineteen people met — nineteen, out of a town that can be walked in an
hour — and they agreed on a thing that was not cruel and was not kind. They agreed to
close the travellers' room. The room went unused. The beds went to other uses. The
food and the fuel and the good blankets were counted out among themselves and kept,
because a severe winter is a thing you can starve in, and nineteen frightened people
did the arithmetic and got an answer they could live with. Underneath the common hearth
the passage was sealed and the fire was banked — kept low and slow and covered, so it
would last, because that was the whole point. Kindness was on the fire's list.
Kindness was what got banked.

**They thought kindness was something they could run out of.**

They were wrong about that, and it is the whole story, and it takes four rooms to say.

**How it plays.** The town is the first thing that feels different, and it never
becomes friendly. It becomes *less careful*. The first sign is not a speech. It is a
door held open that did not used to be held open. The player should notice it and be
allowed to walk past it.

## The sections

Floors are cut by `seed + depth`, not fixed numbers, so these are written as sections:
each one is a *place*, with its own mood, its own animals, its own guardian, and one
answer.

### Section 1 — The Cellar Under the Cottage

**The place.** Flagstone, damp brick, a well-mouth in the corner that nobody in town
admits is a well. Someone cut this stair before the cottage was built, and the cottage
was built around the top of it. The further down you go, the older the stonework gets,
and then the mortar runs out and it is just rock somebody carved steps into.

**The mood.** Close and familiar. Colder than home, but the *shape* of home — somebody
put a shelf here once. You are not far below the kitchen you had tea in. It is the
gentlest place in the game and the first one you are not afraid of. Danger is barely a
word down here. Rats, moths, a spider in a good corner.

**The guardian.** The **Cellar King** — a very large crowned rat who has been holding a
stair shut with a seal for longer than the cottage has stood. It is not clever and it is
not cruel. It is doing a job someone gave it a long time ago and it is tired. *(His
name is not chosen. That one is Rylee's.)*

**The vault.** A ring of old red wax, a small chest, and a note folded on top of the
gems. The note says:

> You made it down. Good. Sit a moment; the stairs are long.
>
> The old tavern kept one room for travellers. The town was afraid of what a kind room
> might cost, so it put a fire over the room, and a seal over the fire, and called that
> safe.
>
> Nothing down here was ever as unkind as the sealing. Take what you found. Take it up.
> Kindness is lighter than it looks, and it knows the way home.

That note is already written and already in the pack (`library/a-note-under-the-seal.md`)
and **it does not change.** It is quoted here exactly.

Also down here, already in the pack: the letter in the chest, tied with thread, signed
with a name you don't know — *I went down on purpose, and I meant to come back. Tell
the keeper I kept my word about the cakes.* (`library/a-letter-in-a-chest.md`.) Quoted
exactly, and it does not change either.

**Whose letter it is.** Not the Spell Keeper's. She came back — the town knows that,
though nobody says it plainly — and this is not her hand, and she would not write *I
meant to come back* about herself. It is **Oren**, the Fisher's brother. He went down
on purpose some years ago, after the Spell Keeper had already returned from her own
descent. Nobody has seen him since. He is not here now, he does not appear, and **the
draft does not say what became of him. Oren's disappearance remains unresolved**, and
the ending does not tidy it.

**The descent's household, which is also down here.** The stair is not a punishment. It
is a job. Three floors down, a great pale moth sleeps on the landing. She has a key. You
wake her; you take the key; she goes back to sleep. Further down, an old mushroom in a
doorway. Further down, a stag beetle in a great deal of armour. Each one is asleep. Each
one is holding something small and important. **This is the shape of the whole descent:
not a gauntlet, a household. Nobody down here wants anything from you.** This is the
game's own floor texture and it is carried into the draft rather than replaced.

**But read the seal note again at the bottom and it does.** In here it sounds like
someone who knows. At the end it will read like someone who has been down this whole
time, saying the only true thing they can, in the dark. That is the cellar's whole job:
it states the thesis in a dead voice, before you know a single name.

**The note and the fire.** The note says the town *put* a fire over the room and a
seal over the fire. Read that again and it does not say something the draft has to
choose between: that is the banking, in the bank's own words. The town deliberately
banked its hearth over the sealed passage to the travellers' room, in the winter, on
purpose, to keep people warm through a severe one.

**The Breeze burned later, by accident. Nobody set it.** No arson, no villain standing
in the snow forty years later, no second meeting. That fire is the same banked fire,
left a little too long untended in a building nobody was using, in a town that had
stopped talking about why. **Deliberate banking, accidental burning** — two events
forty years apart, not one contradiction. The note stands word for word, and it is
simply true.

### Section 2 — The Long Well

**The place.** Under the cellar the rock opens out into something dug on purpose: a
shaft the width of a barn, going down further than a well should, with cisterns cut into
the walls at intervals and little stone staircases spiralling between them. Water
somewhere far below, not falling — just *there*, moving very slowly, like something
breathing in its sleep. Everything echoes once and then does not echo again.

**The mood.** Wet, listening, enormous. Still cold, but the cold of a riverbank at night
rather than a cellar. You feel small and you are fine. Danger rises here for the first
time and it rises as *water* — things that wait and then are simply closer than they
were.

**The guardian.** The **Longneck** — a great grey heron, older than the town, standing
on a ledge in the middle of the shaft with its wings half up. It will not move and it
will not attack you. It is not guarding treasure. It has been standing between the
town's water and everything else since before the sealing, and it will still be doing it
after you leave. Its feathers are a lovely pale grey and it has never once been in a
hurry.

**The vault.** Behind the Longneck's ledge, a room that is not a room: a **bread oven**,
cold, and beside it a ledger in oilcloth. Old Merrin's ledger — he kept The Breeze, and
he wrote down everything the fire needed, in a tidy shopkeeper's hand. Its last page is
the answer to the first thing this town will not say.

**What you read:**

> *Nineteen of us agreed. A fire is only a fire. It does not care what it is over, only
> that it burns, and a hearth is a thing you can count. The winter was the reason. It
> was not the excuse. So we counted, and we closed the room, and we banked the fire to
> last.*

Underneath, in a different, shaking hand: *"Kindness was not on the list."*

The draft does not say whose hand that second line is in, and does not say whether it
is Merrin's or somebody else's. **Merrin went down voluntarily, before the passage was
sealed, on purpose, with his reasons of his own.** He was not appointed to anything. He
was not a jailer, and he did not fail at a job. Nobody sent him and nobody sent him
home. He went, and his coat stayed on its hook, and **his fate remains unresolved.** The
coat is not a clue to a death. It is a clue that somebody still owns it.

**The answer.** The town closed the room on purpose, in a severe winter, and did the
arithmetic out loud and in writing. Then it put a fire over the room and a seal over
the fire, which is what the seal note has been saying all along. The burning came four
decades later and by accident.

### Section 3 — The Banked Fire

**The place.** The stair stops going down and starts going *in*, sideways, into the
flank of something vast and hollow. Chimneys. Soot furring the ceiling like the roof of
a chimney nobody ever came down. Fire-brick, laid by someone who knew what they were
doing. There is heat — real heat, the good kind — and it is the first warm place under
the ground. At the warm end of it, where the passage turns, there is the sealed door.

**The mood.** Ember-orange, sooty, slow. *Rest.* The first floor group in the game that
feels like somewhere you could sit down, and that is exactly when things start to hurt.
This is where "Rising" stops being a suggestion: everything down here lives in the warm
and is comfortable.

**The guardian.** **Old Longsleep** — a vast soft beast, more barrel than animal, curled
on the warm floor and asleep. It has kept the heat for a hundred years by lying on it.
It guards nothing. It is a hearth that learned to breathe and was never told to stop.

**The vault.** A chest built into the wall, in front of the fire, so warm that the
hinges are the coldest thing in the game. Inside: gems, a bar of old silver, **Oren's
net** — a small sad thing, folded, the fisher's brother's — a **coal that will not go
out**, and under it, a note.

**What you read:**

> *"If you are reading this you have got this far, and you are warm, and I am glad. I am
> not going to be able to bring it up myself. Take the coal. Do not let the town put the
> fire out again. Tell them it was never the room's fault."*

**Whose note it is.** Unsigned. **It is not the Spell Keeper's**, because she came back
up. **It is not the writer of the letter in the cellar**, because that letter is in
section 1 and this is section 3, and the draft does not collapse them. Whether it is a
third person's note or one of the two already named and unaccounted for is **not
decided here**, and it is listed in the notes as open. Nobody is written off by it and
nobody is brought back by it.

**The answer.** Somebody went down on purpose to bring the fire back up, meant to come
back, and did not finish. **The coal is theirs** — it is the only thing in the game
that carries the fire home, and it is the mechanical resolve.

### Section 4 — The Blue Door

**The place.** The sealed door at the warm end of the passage. It is an ordinary
cottage door, painted blue once, with a dog bowl beside it that has been full of weather
for forty years. **The coal from section 3 opens it** — the fire opens the door that was
built to hold it in — and then there is the room the town sealed over.

It is **unburnt**. That is the whole reveal and it takes one second. Everyone in town
said there was a fire down here and there was — but the fire was over the *stair*, in the
passage, and never on the room itself. Two beds, both made. A kettle on the hob. A table
laid for six. Bread on the table that is not forty years old.

**The mood.** Still, waiting, and unbearably kind. No horror register anywhere: the
deepest floor of the game is a room where someone set a table. The danger is one tired
animal and the size of your own feeling.

**The guardian.** **Bramble** — the old tavern's dog, a yellow mutt gone grey around the
muzzle, sitting in the exact doorway he was told to sit in. He does not attack you. He
gets up, once, and moves aside, and he looks at the stairs behind you.

**Bramble, exactly.** He was never lost and nobody brought him down here. He is the
dog of the old tavern — The Breeze's own dog — and he has been holding this doorway for
four decades because a man he loved went through it and did not come back, and somebody
had to keep the door. **The draft does not say Merrin is dead.** It says Merrin went
down on purpose before the passage was sealed, that nobody has seen him since, and that
his fate remains unresolved. The dog has been faithful to an unresolved fact for forty
years and has never once needed it resolved. **Bramble comes home.** He comes home with
you, up the same stair, in daylight, and he does not age and he does not suffer, and
afterwards he can walk down to the kept room whenever he likes and sit in one of the six
places. That part matters.

**The vault.** The room itself. There is no chest. The vault is the place, and what is
in it is: the table; a second blanket folded at the foot of the bed; and **Old Merrin's
coat** on a hook, forty years dry.

**What you read:** nothing. There is no note in the vault. The notes were in the cellar
and the banked fire, written by people who came down before you, and everything from
here is a thing you can *pick up and carry*, which is the better part.

**The answer.** What the town sealed away was its own kindness. They were not
protecting themselves from something under the room. They were protecting themselves
from finding out what they had done.

### The kept room, which is the ending

**This is the ending, and it is also the spine of the whole game.**

There is no final boss after the King. There is no speech in the square. There is no
kindness score and no moral explanation and nothing to unlock. **The final section ends
at the Blue Door and the kept room.** There is nothing beyond the Blue Door.

The kept room is the room under the hearth. You unseal it with the coal. It is empty,
and there are **six places set**.

Six. Not five, not a dozen, not a crowd. Six places, laid out, at a table, as though
someone expected six people and is early. You do not find out who they were for. The
draft does not name them. Six is the number, and the six are not explained, because the
room is not a puzzle and the ending is not a ledger.

**The kept room remains revisitable.** This is the part that matters most, and it is the
part that is easiest to get wrong. It is not a cutscene and it is not an unlock. It is a
room you can come back to, as often as you like, forever, with no timer and nothing
counting down.

**Nothing in the kept room ages.** Not the bread, not the blanket, not Merrin's coat on
its hook, and not the dog in the doorway, who has been sitting in it since before you
were born and is not a day older than the day you found him. That is not a trap and not
a loophole; it is simply what the room is. Time does not apply in here. There is no
hidden cost to bringing him through, and no surprise consequence, and no aging-related
harm of any kind. He has a peaceful life at the cottage and he spends part of it in a
room with a table and a fire, and that is the whole of what happened to him.

**The cat's opinion survives.** The hearth-cat has one about all of this, and it is the
same opinion it has about everything, which is that this is your business and it is
beneath the cat. The cat is not a guide. The cat is a cat who permits you to live here,
and permits it on condition that you are occasionally ridiculous. She is allowed to
have the last word about Bramble, and she does, and it is the correct word.

#### The room after the first visit

Once the kept room is open, coming back to it is optional and unhurried. There are at
most **three** signs of welcome across the whole game, one per optional return, and no
more:

1. **First return:** a blanket on the back of a chair that was not there. Nobody says
   where it came from. It is warm.
2. **Second return:** bread on the table, still warm, from a town that is somehow still
   baking. And different people at the table than last time — two of them, this time,
   sitting like they have been there a while. They do not speak to you. They are not
   waiting for you.
3. **Third return:** a small gift on your place. Not a reward. Something someone thought
   was nice — a pressed flower, a coin from a country that is not this one, a child's
   drawing of a room they have never been in.

After the third, the room is simply there. It does not escalate. It does not need you.
This is a sanctuary that gradually welcomes others, and the welcoming is the entire
mechanic. **There are no more than three**, because a fourth is a schedule.

**Hard limits, because the moment these slip the thing stops working.** The kept room
is never: a chore, a check-in, a daily task, a thing with a deadline, a reward for
returning, a shop, a hub with quests in it, a crowd, a hub, or a place the game asks
you to come back to. The player can keep exploring afterwards, forever, and the room
will still be empty and warm and will not ask anything of them.

**The last line of the game is someone making room at the table.**

A cold, wet traveller arrives. It is late and it has been raining and they have nowhere
to be. Nobody makes a speech. Nobody confesses anything. Nobody apologises. Somebody
just pulls out a chair.

> **"There's room."**

That is the ending. The world remains imperfect — there are still people in it who were
not at the meeting, who think you are odd, who never bring it up. The player can close
the game there, or keep playing, or go down the stair again, and the room will be there
when they get back.

## What each depth reveals

Escalates by *place*, never by volume, and never into horror.

1. **The cellar** — this is older than the house. Something is held down, and someone
   down here approves of it.
2. **The Long Well** — the town closed the room, in a winter, on purpose, and did the
   arithmetic. Nineteen people signed for it, and the ledger's own page has the winter
   in it.
3. **The Banked Fire** — someone went down to undo it, on purpose, and did not finish.
   The coal that opens the Blue Door is theirs.
4. **The Blue Door** — it was a room with two beds and a table laid for six, and the
   fire was never on it. The town banked its own hearth because it was afraid of being
   kind, and it could not afford to be kind in a winter.

Each answer arrives twice, in different clothes: once as a thing you read, and once as
a resident's line that is different now that you know. Nobody carries a flag. Miss the
line and you lose a line, not progress. Every note stays in your Library forever,
re-readable, at your pace.

## The final resolve

You take the coal up. That is the whole mechanical resolve and it is deliberately
small: one warm thing in your bag, and the stairs home are long.

The town does not thank you. **The town gets a little warmer, slowly, across many
returns, through small practical acts of hospitality that nobody announces and nobody
explains: a lamp moved to a darker street, a door propped, a pot on that was not on
before.** Improvements are staggered across returns to town rather than triggering
everything simultaneously. The Dwarf Smith takes the coal and his forge is the first
properly warm thing in this town. The town never becomes a good place all at once.

There is no scene in the tavern. Nobody asks the question out loud. Nobody confesses.
The Regular's line changes and that is all he gets, and it is enough:

> *"Merrin. I knew his name. I have known his name since I was nine."*

**Bramble comes up the stairs with you** — the old yellow dog, who has not aged a
day, who walks into a cottage that has a lit hearth in it, and who sleeps in front of
it. And the Spell Keeper, who has kept a cake back every week for forty years
for somebody who came up the stair and was not the one she was keeping it for, puts the
cake down and says:

> *"Sit down."*

And that is not a resolution. That is a room.

The cottage gets its lit window. The town is a little warmer. Nobody fights a final
villain because there was never one — there was a town, and a fire, and a room.

## The part-2 door

The cheapest door that fits, and the one I am asking Rylee to judge the whole story on:

> **Under the hearth-slate in the kind room there is a second stair, and it is warm, and
> nobody in this town has ever once mentioned it.**

Bramble will look at it when you take him up. That is the only omen. Nothing forces it,
nothing names it, and a part 2 needs no new machinery at all — it is the same stair
again, one section further, with a different room at the bottom.

## The two devices, kept

**Residents are lines, not geometry.** Every resident in this draft is a short line said
once, in the room they belong to. Nobody has a quest. Nobody has a schedule the player
can learn. Nobody blocks a door. The town is made out of what people say when you walk
past them, and what they do not say, which is more.

**An object that means more the second time you read it.** The letter is the
workhorse. Read the first time, it is a note from somebody who went missing. Read it
after you have met the Spell Keeper and understood she came back from her own descent
years ago and has kept a cake back every week since, it becomes a promise about
*cakes* — a small, ridiculous, specific promise made in the dark, kept by a man who then
went down anyway. It means more. Nothing about it changed. You did.

The coin, face down, works the other way: the first time it is a coin. The second time,
someone else's habit, someone else's superstition, laid down on purpose and left. It
reads like someone's habit, not yours.

## What this draft deliberately does not say

**One sentence, held back for a part 2: whether anyone the town sealed in ever came back
up.**

The draft says that the Spell Keeper came back. It says that Oren went down and is not
accounted for, and that **Oren's disappearance remains unresolved**. It says that Merrin
went down on purpose before the seal and that **Merrin's fate remains unresolved**. It
sets six places at a table and does not say who they are for.

It never puts those three facts together in prose. It never asks the player to put them
together either. The held-back sentence is the one that would have to come next, and
part 2 is where it belongs — because the answer, when it comes, ought to cost something,
and it cannot cost anything if it is spent in the first hour in a room with six empty
places. And it has to survive the new ending, which ends on *"There's room."* If the
unsaid thing were answered, the six places would become a roster, and the roster would be
a twist, and the ending would be a punchline. It has to stay a table with room at it.

Everything else unresolved stays unresolved. That is not a gap being hidden. That is the
shape.
