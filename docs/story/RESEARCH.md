# Story research

A study of how real dungeon RPGs shape their stories, written 2026-10-08 to stand under the Cottage story draft. **This is a study, not canon.** Nothing here is proposed as story; it is evidence and structure only. Every factual claim about another game carries a link. I mark sources as *primary* (the game's own manual, the developer's own page) or *wiki/secondary* (paraphrase, and sometimes wrong).

**Method note, and it matters:** this worker had no page-fetch tool, so it could not read whole articles — only search-result text from the pages named. Claims below are stated at the confidence a search snippet supports. Where something could not be verified, it is said so in the sentence rather than linked anyway. Treat links as "this page exists and says this much", not "this page has been read end to end".

## Castle of the Winds (Rick Saada, 1989; Epic MegaGames, 1993)

**Premise and why down.** Norse-myth-flavoured: you are in a tiny hamlet, you clear an abandoned mine, and you end up in the fortress near Bjarnhaven, a small town south of it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_of_the_Winds), [StrategyWiki](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Castle_of_the_Winds/Story_and_Locations)). *Wiki/secondary, though Wikipedia and StrategyWiki agree.* The descent is not chosen for a plot reason; it is chosen because that is the shape of the loop — a town to rest and trade in, floors going down.

**What each depth reveals.** Almost nothing is revealed by depth. Each dungeon is a fixed four levels and "features no boss battles or powerful items" ([RogueBasin](https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php/Castle_of_the_Winds), *wiki/secondary*). 40 dungeon levels in the whole game; Part II's castle is 25 levels deep (same source). There is no escalation of revelation — the escalation is *equipment*. This is the single most important fact about CoTW for us.

**How the story is told.** Wikipedia's one-sentence summary is the whole design: the plot is "told with setting changes, unique items, and occasional passages of text." It is told through *objects and places changing*, not through anyone speaking. Three "scraps of parchment" sit at the three key story points ([StrategyWiki, items](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Castle_of_the_Winds/Items_and_the_Inventory_System), *wiki/secondary*); the fourth level of the mine holds one you stop and examine ([GameFAQs FAQ](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/pc/574556-castle-of-the-winds-1-a-question-of-vengeance/faqs/15804)). Those papers are the story's spine, and they are *found by exploring*, never by being told to fetch them.

**The ending, and part 2.** Part I ends with a ruler rewarding you and giving you the run of the castle ([RPGClassics Part II walkthrough](http://shrines.rpgclassics.com/pc/castle2/walkthrough.shtml), *community walkthrough*). Part II then opens by telling you the attack on your hometown was orchestrated by Surtur, lord of Muspelheim ([TVTropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/CastleOfTheWinds), *wiki/secondary*); the deeper bosses are four giant kings, then Surtur ([Wikibooks](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/Castle_of_the_Winds/Story_and_Locations), *wiki/secondary*). **What makes room for part 2 is the threat, not the hero: the attack has a cause, and the cause is still standing.** The manual is the primary source to check for anything load-bearing here ([MOCAGH archive of the manual](https://www.mocagh.org/loadpage.php?getgame=castlewinds)); it could not be opened.

**What you learn about town.** You learn it by trading in it. Bjarnhaven has ten merchants across weapon, armour, general and magic shops, plus a Temple of Odin, a junk store and a sage ([StrategyWiki](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Castle_of_the_Winds/Story_and_Locations), [RogueBasin](https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php/Castle_of_the_Winds)). **No source was found showing the town changing as you descend** — a negative finding, but the evidence points that way: the town's function is economy and rest, not narrative. CoTW buys the player's affection for the town through service, not through change.

## Diablo (Blizzard, 1996)

**Premise and why down.** War between Heaven and Hell; the town of Tristram is under attack by demons pouring out of the ground beneath it ([Diablo Wiki, Fandom](https://diablo.fandom.com/wiki/Diablo_(Game)), *wiki*; corroborated by [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diablo_(video_game))). You go down because that is where the thing making the town afraid is. Four entrances lead from town into the depths: Church, Catacombs, Caves and Hell ([Diablo II wiki's Diablo I page](https://diablo2.diablowiki.net/D1_Tristram), *wiki*).

**What each depth reveals.** You pass through sixteen randomly generated levels and arrive in Hell to face Diablo ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diablo_(video_game))). Escalation is a **worse enemy**, and it is deliberately impersonal — the levels are procedurally generated, so the only consistent "reveal" is the descent itself. The premise is stated as fact to the player from outside: the Prime Evil Diablo was awakened from his Soulstone prison under the town ([DiabloWiki](https://www.diablowiki.net/Diablo_I), *wiki*).

**How the story is told.** Barely, in the space the player walks through. The framing — King Leoric's disappearance, the Cathedral built over the soulstone — exists in the prologue, the ending and the lore, not as things you read on a wall. A source describing Diablo I's in-level narrative delivery **could not be verified**; the strongest evidence is indirect: players asking the developers for a *better prologue* because the story is otherwise not explained ([Reddit thread on the prologue](https://www.reddit.com/r/Diablo/comments/11vyg1j/whichever_wrote_and_directed_the_prologue_give_them/), *forum, weak evidence*). Treat "Diablo tells its story outside the crawl" as a well-supported *inference*, not a quoted fact. A live copy of the Diablo I manual PDF could not be verified either; the Blizzard FTP link circulating on forums is stale.

**The ending, and part 2.** You kill Diablo and the game ends in text. The room it leaves is enormous and cheap: the war between Heaven and Hell was the premise and it is untouched. **Diablo leaves room by refusing to answer** — the boss dies, the frame stays open.

**What you learn about town.** Tristram is a hub with a healer, an inn, an ironmonger and a witch ([Diablo Wiki, Tristram Cathedral](https://diablo.fandom.com/wiki/Tristram_Cathedral), *wiki*). It does not change.

## Eye of the Beholder (Westwood, 1991)

**Premise and why down.** The Lords of Waterdeep hire a four-person party to investigate an evil rising beneath the city; the moment they enter the sewers, the way out is gone ([oldgames.sk](https://www.oldgames.sk/en/game/eye-of-the-beholder), *secondary*). The *primary* source says the same thing in the game's own words: "Once enter the tunnels of Waterdeep, you will soon find that there is no way out" ([SNES manual, scanned PDF](https://www.videogamemanual.com/snes/Advanced%20Dungeons%20%26%20Dragons%20-%20Eye%20of%20the%20Beholder%20(USA).pdf); see also the [DOS manual on MOCAGH](https://www.mocagh.org/ssi/eobgba-manual.pdf)).

**What each depth reveals.** Five sections: Waterdeep Castle, the Sewers, the Dwarven Mines, the Reaches of the Drow, and the surface ([EotB walkthrough with maps](https://www.tlacanada.com/Games/EotB%20Walkthrough.pdf), *community walkthrough*). The reveal is **a bigger world**, and it escalates by *place*: a city's basement becomes a dwarven under-mine becomes drow territory. A friendly dwarf encampment on the fourth level gives you tasks, including finding a missing dwarf prince ([TVTropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/EyeOfTheBeholder), *wiki/secondary*).

**How the story is told.** Mostly by *who is in the dungeon with you*. The frame turns when the party is taken — the captive deep-gnome jester Flutterfoot befriends them and offers to be their guide in Xanathar's lair ([In the Eye of the Beholder](https://www.withadvantage.io/wdh-17-in-the-eye-of-the-beholder/), *review*; also covered in [LPArchive's trilogy playthrough](https://lparchive.org/Eye-of-the-Beholder-Trilogy/Update%2012/)). [Forgotten Realms Wiki](https://forgottenrealms.fandom.com/wiki/Eye_of_the_Beholder_(game)) states the collapse that traps you was arranged by Xanathar (*wiki/secondary*).

**The ending, and part 2.** The room it leaves is a *prisoner problem*: you went down to clear an evil rising, and you cleared it while trapped by the man who arranged it. [CRPG Addict's playthrough](http://crpgaddict.blogspot.com/2015/06/eye-of-beholder-won.html) notes the routes out of the deep are secret and effectively one-way (*secondary playthrough*). **What makes room for part 2 is that the way down was never yours to choose and the way up was never open.**

## Legend of Grimrock (Almost Human, 2012)

**Premise and why down.** "A group of prisoners are sentenced to certain death by exiling them to the secluded Mount Grimrock for vile crimes they may or may not have committed" ([grimrock.net](https://www.grimrock.net/), *primary — the developer's own site*). The down is the sentence. The deeper truth, revealed only at the end, is that the mountain was itself a prison built by the Order of Sacred Fire, holding the Undying One at its core, and it was destroyed when the machine was turned against itself ([Grimrock Wiki: Mount Grimrock](https://grimrock.fandom.com/wiki/Mount_Grimrock), *wiki/secondary*).

**What each depth reveals.** This is the cleanest escalation of the six, and it is **a personal answer**: the dungeon is not a place, it is a sentence being served, and the reveal at the bottom is about what the sentence was *for*. Twelve levels; level 12 is literally the Prison ([Classic Games Revisited walkthrough](https://classicgames.sorcerers.net/grimrock1/), *community walkthrough*).

**How the story is told.** **The best single mechanic in this study.** Toorum, a previous occupant of the mountain, "has left sixteen notes all throughout the dungeon. You'll need a sharp eye and an explorer's attitude to find them" ([GameBanshee: Toorum's Notes](https://www.gamebanshee.com/legendofgrimrock/toorumsnotes.php), *community guide*). And Toorum himself is the ending: he met his fate on level 10, sealed inside a wall, where the player finds his remains **and his final two notes** ([Grimrock Wiki: Toorum](https://grimrock.fandom.com/wiki/Toorum), *wiki*). A dead predecessor's notes are the worldbuilding, the tutorial, and the last thing you read. Note also how *notes* are the story channel: the standard walkthrough explicitly "does not recite the various notes" ([Classic Games Revisited](https://classicgames.sorcerers.net/grimrock1/)) — the notes are the game, not a transcription of it.

**The ending, and part 2.** Wikipedia's summary of the final scenes: the party runs down a stone hallway, and "the final fate of the prisoners seemingly unknown" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legend_of_Grimrock)). **What makes room is the unanswered question about the protagonists** — and the fact that this was sequelised, with a second game set on the Isle of Nex ([Steam, LoG II](http://store.steampowered.com/app/251730/)).

**What you learn about town.** There is no town. Grimrock drops the hub entirely, and the story stays cheap to build — but it loses the thing Cottage already has.

## Ultima Underworld: The Stygian Abyss (Blue Sky/Origin, 1992)

**Premise and why down.** Accused of kidnapping a princess and thrown into the dungeons to prove your innocence ([LP, episode 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RD5y7dXt3dw), *secondary*). The manual and guide are the primary source ([MOCAGH archive](https://www.mocagh.org/loadpage.php?getgame=uuw); a compilation of the manual PDFs is at [Bootstrike](http://bootstrike.com/Uw1/downloads.php)); they could not be opened.

**What each depth reveals.** Eight levels ([RPG Gamers walkthrough](https://rpggamers.com/walkthrough/ultima-underworld), *community walkthrough*; [Ultima Codex](https://wiki.ultimacodex.com/wiki/Ultima_Underworld:_The_Stygian_Abyss)). The escalation is **both**: bigger place and worse enemy, with a floor 8 flooded with lava and the worst monsters in the game ([Ultima Codex walkthrough](https://wiki.ultimacodex.com/wiki/Ultima_Underworld_walkthrough)). But the *story* reveal is a **personal answer**, and it is delivered in fragments by an NPC: talk to the Knights on level 4 repeatedly and "each vision gives you 2 letters of the 6-letter mantra" ([GameFAQs walkthrough](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/pc/562665-ultima-underworld-the-stygian-abyss/faqs/1811)). Two letters at a time, repeatedly, from a stranger — that is a story machine, not a cutscene.

**How the story is told.** Dialogue with dungeon residents, and objects. The Chamber of Virtue and the Talismans on level 8 ([Gamer Walkthroughs](https://gamerwalkthroughs.com/ultima-underworld/level-8/)) are the game's version of "an object that changes meaning": the same kind of object, eight times, doing eight different jobs.

**The ending.** The dungeon is a failed utopia — a colony built in the Abyss to promote Virtue that fell ([Ultima Fandom](https://ultima.fandom.com/wiki/Ultima_Underworld:_The_Stygian_Abyss), *wiki, and note that the "Virtue" framing is later-series lore rather than 1992 text*). **The specific mechanics of the final fight and escape could not be verified** from any source reached; treat that part as unverified.

**What you learn about town.** Castletown at the surface, and — this is the part that matters — the game is *about* the surface while you are far from it. That framing is why it is one of the few examples that feels like a home you are away from.

## Delve (open-source roguelike, CC0)

Included because it is the closest public example to Cottage's mechanics: a turn-based, no-reflex dungeon crawl with a town loop. The developer's own page describes it as "an open source game released under the Creative Commons CC0 license", inspired by NetHack, Angband and Brogue ([Dana Jansens on itch.io](https://danarama.itch.io/delve), *primary*). **Honest limitation: almost nothing about Delve's story structure was verifiable** — its wiki coverage is thin and search results were flooded with Path of Exile's "Delve" and unrelated titles. A browser port at [goonk.se](https://goonk.se/delve/) describes an "Aldric, last Keeper of the Deep vault", but **whether that is a port of Dana Jansens' Delve rather than a different game could not be verified**, so it is not cited as evidence. What is worth taking from Delve is a *mechanic*, not a claim about the game: CC0 source means the pattern is directly copyable in spirit.

# Synthesis: how dungeon RPGs earn a story

## The moves they converge on

Six games, six moves. These are the shapes that recur, and they are worth naming precisely because Cottage can pick deliberately rather than by accident.

**1. The story is an artifact, not a speech.** All of them tell you about the world through things you pick up and read: CoTW's three scraps of parchment, Grimrock's sixteen notes from a dead explorer, EotB's secret doors and the jester's guide, UW's talismans. Wikipedia's summary of CoTW — "setting changes, unique items, and occasional passages of text" — is the whole genre's thesis. **Nobody in this study delivers plot through a quest log, because none of them has one.**

**2. The premise is delivered in the first minute, before the player can act.** Waterdeep's exit collapses the instant you enter ([manual](https://www.videogamemanual.com/snes/Advanced%20Dungeons%20%26%20Dragons%20-%20Eye%20of%20the%20Beholder%20(USA).pdf)). You are sentenced to Mount Grimrock ([grimrock.net](https://www.grimrock.net/)). Tristram is being eaten ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diablo_(video_game))). The player then spends hours descending, and the only thing that changes is what they know. One sentence, up front, that the whole descent is about.

**3. Depth reveals a *category*, not a number.** Sewers → dwarven mines → drow territory (EotB). Four unremarkable mine levels → Bjarnhaven's castle → a 25-deep fortress (CoTW). Eight Abyss levels (UW). The genre almost never escalates by "the floor is harder"; it escalates by "the floor *is* somewhere else".

**4. There is a predecessor.** CoTW's scraps come from someone before you. Grimrock's Toorum. UW's failed colony. This is the cheapest, strongest story move available: **the dungeon is not new, it is inhabited by the traces of the last person who came down** — and it makes every found note a relationship rather than a fact.

**5. The ending leaves an open door, and there are only three kinds of open door.** An unresolved question (Grimrock: unknown fate; Diablo: Heaven and Hell still at war). A surviving enemy (CoTW: Surtur is named in Part I and fought in Part II). A place you were trapped in rather than chose (EotB).

**6. Almost nobody changes the town.** This is the finding that most directly shapes Cottage. Across five games, the hub is economy, healing and rest. UW is the only one that feels like a home, and it gets that from *being absent and talked about*, not from being rebuilt. **If Cottage's story lives in the town, it is going somewhere no example here went.** That is an argument for doing it anyway, and an argument for doing it cheaply — with changed lines, not changed maps.

## What fits this game's soul, and what would fight it

The soul: curious, low-bandwidth exploring; the thrill of opening something and finding something fun; cozy storybook; no reflexes, nothing to memorize.

**Fits:** artifact-as-story (move 1) — it *is* the Library ruleset, already built. Premise-in-one-sentence (move 2) — one note under the cottage door, already drafted. Predecessor (move 4) — a letter signed with a name you do not know is already in the pack and is the purest example in this study. Category-escalation (move 3) — "sections of floors each with its own place" is exactly CoTW's hamlet→mine→castle and EotB's sewer→mine→drow.

**Would fight the soul:** anything requiring a quest log, a quest *marker*, or a "return to the Keeper" flag the player must notice. The one exception is a flag that *removes a thing* — Rylee already has `the-keeper-knows`, where reading the letter makes a resident say one line she would not otherwise say. That is fine, because nothing is *owed*; missing it costs a line, not progress. Also fighting the soul: real-time, reflex combat; a language-cipher gate on notes (Grimrock's glyph-translation is a lovely idea and would fight "nothing to memorize" — skip the cipher, keep the *feeling* of a note that means more once you've been deeper); and a horror register. Grimrock and UW are great studies and are the wrong *temperature*.

## Delivering a story to a player who will not read a quest log

The player is exploring because she is curious, and curiosity means she walks into the corner she did not need to walk into. So:

- **Every story beat must be attachable to something she wanted anyway.** The letter is in an open chest she opened for the ring. The tavern's tab is behind the counter of the shop she is standing in. Neither asks for a detour; both pay out a detour she chose.
- **The beat must not expire.** If a story fact only exists as a talk option she has to remember to take, it is lost. Every note is re-readable from the Library, permanently, at her pace. That is the entire reason the Library is the first ruleset.
- **The same fact should arrive at least twice in different clothes.** Once as an object, once as a person's changed line. CoTW's town doesn't change; Rylee's already-picked plan for the Keeper is better than CoTW's. Reading the letter and then hearing the Keeper say one new line is the strongest delivery in the pack's existing notes, because the *object's meaning changes* when she reacts to it.
- **Give one answer per section, and let the town hold the wrong answer meanwhile.** The tavern is "cozy but wary of you" — the town not knowing is the story's engine, and it costs nothing to write because warier is the default.

## The thrill of opening something, made of what

Rylee named it: "opening and finding something fun". Broken into parts, from these games:

1. **A closed container with a reason to want it** (chest, safe, sealed vault, the room behind the seal).
2. **A one-second, non-skippable presentation.** CoTW and Diablo both make the reveal the reward; DESIGN.md already commits to instant-with-motion-off, spin-with-motion-on, rarity shown immediately, traits hidden until identified. That is the mechanic.
3. **Some contents are story.** Not every drop. Three or four per section are the truth-bearing objects — a letter, a page, a tab, a name. They are in the same chest format as the good loot, so the thrill that carries the story is the thrill she already came for. **This is the whole trick: the story rides the loot, it never asks for its own container.**
4. **Some contents mean more later.** The second reading of a note after a truth is the payoff. An object that changes meaning is the single most reused device in this study — CoTW's unique items, UW's talismans, Grimrock's Toorum notes.

## A recommended shape for Cottage

Not story. Structure, that Rylee's words can be poured into. The three beats — two truths and a realization, with the Spell Keeper as the one who went down on purpose and is in town — are Rylee's picks as recorded in `NOTES.md` and `docs/story-spine-notes.md`, not the researcher's invention; what follows is where they can live.

**Town.** Small, warm, wary. It is the *only* place that changes, and it changes by lines, not by geometry: each resident has one line per truth known. The tavern's tab is behind the counter. The Keeper is the one person who is already glad you came back down. Town is where the story's score is displayed without a quest log — walk past the Regular and her line is simply different now.

**Sections.** Sections, not a single deepening shaft: floors grouped into sections, each section one *place* with its own mood and its own escalation category (CoTW's hamlet → mine → castle, at four floors a pop, is the proven scale). Section 1 is the cellar you already have. Each new section earns its own colour of monster, its own tile set, and its own answer.

**Guardian and vault.** Each section ends with a named guardian and a vault it opens. That is the genre's cleanest beat and it matches what is already built (Keybearers, keys on stairs, the sealed door into the King's room). The guardian is a *place-marker with a person attached*: it is what the player says when asked what is down there. The vault is where the section's truth-object sits, beside ordinary treasure, in the same chest format.

**Delivery order per section:** one premise note near the top → ordinary exploring → one truth-object in a chest → guardian → vault with the answer. Then walk home. The Keeper's line is the receipt.

**The final resolve.** Bottom of the last section: a guardian whose seal opens a room, a treasure, a lore note carrying the realization, and a stair home — the shape Rylee already chose on 2026-10-02. Then the part-2 door, chosen from the three verified kinds: **an unresolved question is the cheapest and the most on-soul.** CoTW's surviving-enemy door requires a whole second villain; EotB's trapped door is closed by the time you arrive. For Cottage, leave one thing unsaid in the realization note — the sealed thing is *also* something else, still down there, still warm — and the part 2 needs no new machinery, because the next section of floors is the same machinery again.

**What could not be verified, and would be wanted before drafting:** the text of CoTW's own ending and Part II opening (manual not opened); Diablo I's in-level narrative delivery and the ending text; Ultima Underworld's final level mechanics; anything about Delve's story. Of these, only the CoTW manual and the UW manual matter enough to be worth someone opening them; the rest would not be built on.
